Onboarding & Joining Flow for Developers

High-level summary of Bootstrapping a Verifiable Trust Community (V1, 2025-12-19), framed for a kernel-style developer community.
Why this exists. This is a condensed, high-level view of the source design, prepared for the team evaluating the best onboarding experience on the VTI stack. It keeps only the joining flow and the major components — deep credential mechanics are intentionally left out. The source's example policies are explicitly modeled on what a Linux-kernel-style community wants, which is why it maps cleanly to developer onboarding.

The goal is a community where every developer is a verified, real person, relationships between developers are cryptographically vouched, and there are clear rules for who can join and who can invite. Unlike an open sign-up (where anyone can spin up fake accounts), membership is earned through layered trust — nobody joins without being vouched for.

The core idea: trust grows in layers

The community expands outward from a single origin, and each layer has stronger joining requirements than the last. This keeps the community secure while still allowing organic, self-service growth once it's established.

The four phases of joining

Onboarding is not one flow — it evolves as the community matures. Each phase unlocks a new tier of people who are allowed to invite, and a new bar for newcomers to clear.

1

Genesis — the community is created

One founder instantiates the community and automatically becomes the first member. They set the initial rules for who can join. No onboarding of others yet — this is the seed.

2

Founder invites Trust Anchors

The founder invites a small, hand-picked group of trusted people (the maintainers who will grow the community responsibly). They exchange verified credentials to confirm the relationship. Bar to join: a direct invitation from the founder.

3

Trust Anchors invite Members

Anchors can now bring in developers. A newcomer needs an invitation from one anchor and a vouched connection with at least one other anchor. This is where the "web of trust" begins — more than one person must stand behind a newcomer.

4

Members invite Members (organic growth)

Regular members can now invite others, so the community self-sustains. The bar is highest here: one invitation, vouched connections with at least two other members, and professional identity verification (an independent ID check).

A developer's join journey

Below is the steady-state experience for a new developer (call them Bob) joining an established community — the Phase 4 flow, which is the one researchers should optimize for since it's how most people will actually onboard.

  VTC->>VTC: Checks rules & registry
  VTC->>Bob: Issues membership → Bob is now a member
  Note over Bob,VTC: Bob can now invite others (loop continues)

The key experience insight for the VTI stack: joining is a gather-then-present flow. A newcomer collects a few things — an invite, a couple of peer vouches, and an ID check — then presents them once, and the community's rules engine decides automatically. There is no manual gatekeeper approving each person; the policy does the work.

Major components

These are the building blocks the VTI stack needs to support. Grouped by role — who the actors are, what gets exchanged, and the machinery that enforces the rules.

Actors

FTA

Founding Trust Anchor

The person who starts the community and becomes its first member. Seeds the initial rules.

CTA

Community Trust Anchor

A trusted maintainer, invited by the founder, allowed to bring in new members.

VCM

Verifiable Community Member

A developer who was invited, met the rules, and holds a membership credential.

IdV

Identity Verifier

An independent, commercial ID-proofing provider (e.g. the "background check" step in Phase 4).

What developers hold & exchange

M-DID

Member Identity

A developer's unique verifiable identity within the community — the anchor for everything else they hold.

Invitation

Invite Credential

Proof that an existing member invited this person to join (the CIC/VIC pair in the source).

Vouch

Peer Relationship Credential

A cryptographic "I know and trust this person" link — the threads of the web of trust (VRC).

ID Proof

Identity Credential

Result of the professional identity check, required at the Phase 4 tier (IDVC).

Membership

Membership Credential

The community's verifiable "you are a member" credential, issued once the rules are met (VMC).

Community machinery

PEP

Policy Engine

Holds and enforces the joining rules — decides automatically whether a newcomer qualifies.

TR

Trust Registry

The record of who is a member and what role they hold; consulted when verifying anyone.

Protocols

Exchange & Policy Protocols

Standard flows for exchanging vouches between peers and for updating the community's rules.

Takeaways for the VTI-stack experience study

  • Optimize the Phase 4 flow. It's the steady-state path most developers will take, and it has the most steps (invite → vouches → ID check → present). Friction here defines the onboarding experience.
  • The hard UX problem is "gather-then-present." A newcomer must collect several credentials from different parties before the single join moment. Making that collection feel guided rather than like a scavenger hunt is the core experience challenge.
  • Vouching is social, not technical. Requiring 2+ peer vouches means onboarding depends on a newcomer already having relationships. The experience needs to help newcomers find and request vouches.
  • Rules do the gatekeeping, not people. Acceptance is automatic once proofs check out — so the experience should make the requirements transparent up front ("here's exactly what you still need").
  • Identity can stay pseudonymous. The ID check confirms a real person exists without necessarily exposing a legal name to the community — relevant for how much a kernel developer must reveal.
  • Note the source's open items: where the community "lives" (local founder machine → hosted service) and how identity credentials are actually trusted were still marked TODO in the original — worth flagging as open design questions.
